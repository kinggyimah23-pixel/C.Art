01 / THE PHOTOGRAPHER

A life in pictures.

More than a photographer, Barnor became a witness to social change — recording everyday lives as well as moments that entered history.

Ever Young Graphic Studio · Jamestown, Accra · 1956

From Accra, to London, and back again.

James Barnor was born in Accra, Ghana, in 1929. He received his first camera, a Kodak Baby Brownie, as a teenager. What began as curiosity became a lifelong commitment to photography.

After learning the craft through an apprenticeship, Barnor opened Ever Young Studio in Jamestown in 1949. The studio became a place where people came to be photographed, meet and share stories. His portraits captured the confidence, style and aspirations of a society moving towards independence.

He also worked as a photojournalist and became the first staff photographer of the Daily Graphic. His camera documented public life and important personalities during a defining period in Ghana's history.

In 1959, Barnor moved to London to develop his technical skills. He studied photography and worked for DRUM magazine, photographing fashion, culture and the African diaspora. In 1970, he returned to Ghana and helped establish a colour-processing laboratory, continuing his work at Studio X23.

He returned to London in 1994. His archive, spanning Ghana and Britain, is now recognised internationally for its historical, cultural and artistic importance.

“The future is going to be grander because of photography.”
— JAMES BARNOR · QUOTED BY MoMA

04 / CONTRIBUTIONS

More than a camera.

Barnor's contribution is visible in the photographs he made, the photographic practices he helped develop and the histories he preserved.

01 / STUDIO

Ever Young Studio

Established in Jamestown, Accra, in 1949. It became a portrait studio and a lively social space for people in the community.

02 / PHOTOJOURNALISM

Daily Graphic

Served as the newspaper's first staff photographer, documenting public life and important figures in Ghana.

03 / HISTORY

Independence record

Photographed Ghana's transition to independence, preserving visual records of people and events around 1957.

04 / EDITORIAL

DRUM magazine

Created fashion and documentary images that captured Black life, style and the African diaspora in 1960s Britain.

05 / TECHNOLOGY

Colour processing

Helped establish Ghana's first colour-processing laboratory after returning in 1970, broadening access to colour photography.

06 / ARCHIVE

A visual record

Built an extensive body of work across Ghana and Britain, documenting social change through portrait, street and fashion photography.

06 / THE LEGACY

He photographed a changing world — and made sure it would be remembered.

James Barnor's archive is a record of ordinary people and extraordinary transitions. His portraits show how people wished to be seen; his documentary photographs reveal the energy of cities and communities; his fashion images capture changing ideas of style and identity.

His work matters because it places Ghanaian lives and African diasporic experiences at the centre of photographic history. It reminds us that history is not only made by leaders and major events. It is also made by families, workers, young people, artists and communities.

His photographs continue to inspire new generations of photographers and viewers.
